Autoclave

  • An autoclave is a device which works under high pressure.
  • It was invented by Charles Chamberland in 1879.
  • It works by heating the material at a definite temperature which is above its boiling point at normal atmospheric pressure to achieve sterilization. It is also named as steam sterilizer. This is based on the method of humid heat sterilization; it is the most effective and reliable way of sterilization.
  • This is used in industries for cure coating, hypothermal synthesis, and in research experiments for the sterilization of the instrument and culture media.

Principle

It creates saturated vapour at a pressure of 15 pounds/sq. inch to generate 250°F (121°C) temperature for 30–60 mins. Humid heat kills all microbes by disrupting cell membranes, denaturing enzymes, degradation of nucleic acid and other vital proteins. To kill all microbes ten minutes exposure of boiling water is enough; killing of heat-resistant endospores needs more heating than boiling point of water. This will be done by increasing the inner pressure of the autoclave.

Construction / Components of Autoclave

  • Pressure Chamber: It is the main component of a steam autoclave consisting of inner chamber and an outer jacket (iron case). The size of the chamber ranges from 100L to 300L.
  • Lid / Door: The purpose is to seal off the outside atmosphere and create sterilized condition on heat inside of the autoclave. The lid is made airtight via the screw clamps and asbestos washer. It consists of various components like pressure gauge, whistle, and safety valve.
  • Pressure Gauge: A pressure gauge is present on the lid of autoclave to indicate the pressure created in the autoclave during sterilization. It is essential as it assures the safety of the autoclave and working condition of the operation.
  • Pressure Releasing Unit / Whistle: The whistle is placed on top of the surface of the lid, just like pressure cooker. The whistle allows us to release the pressure whenever required.
  • Safety Valve: It is present on the surface of the lid; its function is to avoid catastrophic accident especially when pressure inside the chamber is uncontrolled.
  • Heater: The electric heater is placed beneath the chamber. The electric heater working is similar to geyser.

Working

  1. Before beginning to use the autoclave, it should be checked for any items left from previous cycle.
  2. A sufficient amount of water is then put inside the chamber.
  3. Now, the materials to be sterilized are placed inside the chamber. Don't autoclave materials that catch fire easily; combustible material like paper should be in secondary object/autoclave bag.
  4. The lid is then closed, and screws are tightened to ensure an airtight condition & electric heater is switched on. The safety valves are adjusted to maintain the required pressure in the chamber.
  5. Once the water inside chamber boils, the air-water mixture is allowed to escape through discharge tube to let all air inside be displaced.
  6. Drainage pipe is then closed & steam is allowed to reach the desired level (15 lbs in most cases).
  7. Once pressure is reached, the whistle blows to remove excess pressure from the case.
  8. Now, electric heater is switched off and the autoclave is allowed to cool until pressure gauge indicates the pressure inside has lowered down to that of atmospheric pressure.
  9. The discharge pipe is then opened to allow entry of air from outside into autoclave.
  10. Finally, the lid is opened and the sterilized materials are taken out of the chamber.

Application

  • They are absolutely essential for ensuring sterile environment in location of medical and healthcare facilities, for sterilizing surgical instruments, medical equipment, dressing leading to removal of harmful substances.
  • In laboratories and research institutions, essential for many applications that are required to maintain aseptic things, sterility of the growth media necessary for culture of cell and analytical studies.
  • Used in food & beverage industries for ensuring desired hygiene standards through maintenance of the safe material.

Precautions

  • There should be sufficient space inside the autoclave after keeping all material for sterilization.
  • The material should be kept inside secondary container.
  • Do NOT keep combustible materials like papers; keep them in an autoclave bag to prevent fire.
  • Do NOT use plastic trays or containers as they might melt and damage the autoclave.
  • The liquid inside the container should be 2/3rd of total volume. This will avoid the spilling of the liquid.
  • Do NOT open the lid when the autoclave is working.

Physical Methods of Sterilization

There are many kinds of approaches done to sterilize the material:

  • A) Moist Heat: Culture media and water are sterilized by using moist heat i.e. steam under pressure. It is done through autoclaving and also by using a pressure cooker.
    • Autoclaving is the most effective and most efficient means of sterilization.
    • Standard parameters: 121°C / 15 psi for 15 min.
  • B) Dry Heat: Produced by hot air oven. Glassware (syringes, forceps, scalpel, pipette, flask, petri dish, etc.) are sterilized at 160°C/2 hours or 170°C/1 hour. Care should be taken to remove these apparatuses only when temperature cools down.
  • C) Incineration: Process of killing microorganisms using flame (flame sterilization). Done by holding inoculation needle, loop & tip of forceps into Bunsen burner flame till red hot.

Hot Air Oven

A hot air oven is an electrical device that uses dry heat to sterilize material that can resist heat, thereby removing microorganisms such as bacterial spores from the object placed inside its chamber. It has origins in Pasteur's work.

Principle

Combines conduction, convection, and radiation mechanics. Conduction is the main factor. Heat is gradually transmitted layer by layer as exterior surface absorbs it until the item reaches required sterilizing temperature.

Components / Construction of Hot Air Oven

  • Cabinet: Outermost layer of protection made of stainless steel or aluminium, resistant to oxidation & robust against impact.
  • Fiber Glass Insulation: Layer of glass wool insulation between inner chamber and outer cabinet.
  • Chamber: Rectangular inner chamber made of stainless steel or aluminium with ribs for shelves.
  • Shelves: Aluminium built shelves to store objects.
  • Motorized Fans / Blowers: Ensures equal dispersion of heat throughout the chamber.
  • Power Supply: Transformer and rectifier supplying 220V, 50Hz.
  • Thermostat: Connected directly to heater & heat sensor to regulate temperature.

Laminar Flow Hood

An enclosed workbench designed to create a microbial-free work environment through an installed filter in the cabinet so that most particles are prevented from entering.

Principle / Working

Based on laminar flow of air through HEPA filters. Air first passes through filter pad, then blower/fan directs it toward HEPA filter, which traps bacteria and fungi.

Types of Laminar Flow Hood

  • Vertical Laminar Flow Cabinet: Air moves from top to bottom. Requires less floor space.
  • Horizontal Laminar Flow Cabinet: Air comes from behind and projects outward toward user. Parallel airflow cleanses workspace with constant velocity.

Construction / Parts

  • Cabinet: Stainless steel body with a front glass shield.
  • Fan / Blower: Sucks air into cabinet.
  • UV Lamp: Germicidal, sterilizes interior before use.
  • Fluorescent Lamp: Light during operation.
  • Working Station: Area where experiment is conducted.
  • Filter Pad: Pre-filter on top to catch dust/large particles.

Precautions for Hood

  • UV sterilization should be done before and after experiment.
  • Do NOT use UV light and airflow simultaneously.
  • UV lamp should not be on for a long period.

LB MEDIA PREPARATION AND STERILIZATION

  • Luria Broth (LB) Media
  • Luria Agar (LA) Media

Theory

History:

LB is widely used bacterial culture medium today but it has its origins in the field of bacteriophage genetics. Giuseppe Bertani created the LB recipe while trying to optimize plaque formation on a Shigella indicator strain.

According to Bertani, LB media has been variously misconstrued to stand for "Luria Broth", "Luria-Bertani" medium, and "Lennox Broth"; however, the acronym originally stood for "Lysogeny Broth".

The agar form of the medium should be designated LA but it is often referred to as LB. Although originally developed for bacteriophage studies and Shigella growth, LB subsequently became the medium of choice for growth of Escherichia coli and other related enteric species.

Sterilization and the Autoclave

When microbiological media has been made, it still has to be sterilized because of microbial contamination from air, glassware, hands etc. Within a few hours there will be thousands of bacteria reproducing in the media so it has to be sterilized quickly before the microbes start using the nutrient up.

The sterilization process is a 100% kill, and guarantees that the medium will stay sterile unless exposed to contaminants by less than adequate aseptic technique to exposure to air.

Media sterilization is carried out with the autoclave, basically a huge steam cooker. Steam enters into the jacket surrounded the chamber. When the pressure from the steam at a certain point in the jacket, a valve allows the steam to enter the chamber.

The pressure will go up over 15 pounds per square inch (Psi); at this point the timer begins to count down — usually for 15 mins, depending on this type of media. The high pressure in a closed container allows the temperature to go above the highest temp one could get by just boiling, around 121°C.

Therefore, the parameter for sterilization with an autoclave are 121°C at >15 psi for 15 min. 15 minutes is the thermal death time for most organism (except some really hardy spore formers).

Growth of E. Coli Cells in LB Medium

Growth Curve:

It is a graphical representation of how particular quantity increases over time.

Bacterial Growth Curve: No. of live cells in a bacterial population over a period of time.

It has 4 Phases:

  1. Lag Phase:
    • Initial Phase
    • Cellular activity but no growth.
    • Adaptation
    • Cell increases in size but no cell division.
  2. Exponential Phase:
    • Cell division by binary fission.
    • Metabolic activity increases (DNA, RNA etc.) growth phase.
    • Utilize nutrients fully.
  3. Stationary Phase:
    • Nutrient depletion
    • No. of dividing cells = No. of cells dying
    • No overall growth
  4. Death / Decline Phase:
    • Nutrient decline
    • No. of dying bacteria increases
    • Population decreases

Purpose

LB medium is a rich medium that is commonly used to culture membrane of the Enterobacteriaceae as well as for coliphage plaque assays. LB and related media (SOC, Terrific Broth, 2xYT, etc.) are used extensively in recombinant DNA work and other molecular biology procedures. Often an antibiotic is added to the sterilized medium to select for cells that contain a specific genetic element such as a plasmid, a transposon, or a gene disruption via an antibiotic resistance cassette. X-Gal (5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl-beta-D-galactoside) may be added to sterile medium when using the blue-white screen for plasmids bearing the alpha fragment of the beta-galactosidase gene (a complementation analysis).

IPTG (Isopropyl-beta-D-thiogalactopyranoside) is sometimes added to induce expression of gene controlled by the lac promoter.

In addition to (or perhaps because of) its prominent position in the molecular biological field, LB has also been used as a general purpose bacterial culture medium for a variety of facultative organisms.

In the undergraduate microbiology teaching labs, LB is sometimes used as the growth surface when attempting to analyze bacterial colony morphology.

Composition of 1 Liter Media Preparation

  • Tryptone: 10g
  • Yeast Extract: 5g
  • NaCl: 10g

Dissolve components in 1L of distilled or deionized water. For LB agar add agar to final conc. of 1.5%. Heat the mixture to boiling to dissolve agar and sterilize by autoclave at 15 psi, from 121-124°C for 15 min.

Media Distribution

The prepared media is distributed in different ways, depending on the form one is making. Broth and agar deeps are dispensed into tubes and then sterilized. Agar slant tubes are sterilized and then the rack tilted to allow the agar to solidify in a slanted fashion. Agar medium to be poured into plates is sterilized via an autoclave.

Certain high protein solutions such as vaccines, and the serum will denature in the extreme heat, and so they may have to be filter sterilized without heat. You will be making slant and broth media, but not plate media in this lab.

E. Coli Bacterium

  • E. Coli (Escherichia coli) is a type of bacteria that normally lives in intestines. It's also found in the gut of some animals and even helps keep the digestive tract healthy.
  • Some strains of E. coli can cause diarrhea if you eat contaminated food or drink fouled water.
  • E. Coli O157:H7 strain causes severe food poisoning. It causes abdominal cramps, vomiting, and bloody diarrhea. It is the leading cause of acute kidney failure in children. It can also cause life-threatening symptoms.
  • We can also get pneumonia and urinary tract infection from different types of the bacterial strain. In fact, 75-95% of urinary tract infections are caused by E. coli.
  • Some types of E. coli make you sick by making a toxin called Shiga. Toxin damages the lining of your intestine. The strains of E. coli that make the toxin are sometimes called STEC "shiga toxin-producing E.coli".
  • It is gram-negative, facultative anaerobic, rod-shaped, coliform bacterium of the genus Escherichia that is commonly found in the lower intestine of warm-blooded organisms.

Chromosomal or Genomic D.N.A

  • Chromosomal DNA is the genomic DNA (gDNA) found in prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms.
  • Eukaryotic genomes possess a few linear chromosomes, whereas prokaryotic genomes carry a single circular chromosome.
  • In eukaryotes, they are double-stranded and contain an origin of replication. The presence of more than one origin of replication in eukaryotes is credited to their larger size. The chromosomal number of particular types varies in species.
  • Most of the species are diploid containing two copies of a particular type of chromosome. As it represents the genome of an individual, it is essential for the growth, evolution, and reproduction of an individual.

Roles of Chemicals with the E. coli Cells

  • GTE (Glucose, Tris Buffer, EDTA):
    • Glucose: It maintains the osmolarity of the cell.
    • Tris Buffer: It maintains the pH of the cell and surrounding.
    • EDTA: Acts as chelating agent & maintains integrity of cell membrane.
  • SDS: It helps for the cell lysis & solubilize the proteins of the cell membrane.
  • NaCl: Provide salt to genomic DNA, coating of DNA and helps for precipitation of DNA.
  • BPB Dyes: Helps to track the DNA during gel electrophoresis.
  • Ethidium Bromide: EtBr binds with DNA molecule and illuminate under the UV light, the DNA banding pattern can be visualized. Ethidium bromide (also known as homidium bromide) is an intercalating agent commonly used as a fluorescent tag (nucleic acid stain) during agarose gel electrophoresis.

Procedure: Extraction of gDNA from E. coli

Take 1.5 ml of overnight grown culture in a microfuge tube
Centrifuge at 8,000 rpm for 5 min
Discard the supernatant
Add 200 µl of GTE mix to the pellet
Break the pellet by knuckling
Incubate at room temperature for 5 min
Add 400 µl of 1% SDS
Keep on ice for 5 min
Add 60 µl of 10mM NaCl (1/10)
Add 700 µl of ice cold isopropanol (DNA visible) and invert mix
Centrifuge at 10,000 rpm for 20 min
Decant the alcohol and air dry the pellet
Dissolve in 50–100 µl distilled water (volume of water depends upon the thickness of the pellet)
Add 6 µl BPB dye and perform Agarose Gel Electrophoresis

Precautions

  • Use caution when operating the centrifuge.
  • The laboratory surfaces should be very clean during all procedures used in this activity.
  • Use thoroughly clean instruments and glassware. Rinse all equipment with isopropanol alcohol or acetone.

Transformation:

A bacterium takes up a piece of DNA from its environment is known as transformation.

Competence:

  • Transformation Efficiency is defined as the number of colony forming units (cfu) which would be produced by transforming 1 µg of plasmid into a given volume of competent cell.
  • Competent cells are ready to use bacterial cells that possess more easily altered cell walls by which foreign DNA can be passed through easily. Most types of cells cannot take up DNA efficiently unless they have been exposed to special chemical or electrical treatment to make them competent cells.

There are two types of competence:

  1. Natural competence
  2. Artificial competence

(i) Natural competence:

Bacteria are able to take up DNA from their environment by three ways: conjugation, transformation, and transduction. In transformation, the DNA is directly entered to cell. Uptake of transforming DNA requires the recipient cell to be in a specialized physiological state called competent state.

Algorithm: Transformation Efficiency

  • Calculation of the number of colonies grown on culture medium.
  • Find the total number of colonies which may grow after culturing the whole volume of sample which is being transferred.
  • Calculate the amount of plasmid DNA which is used for transforming.
  • Calculate the transformation efficiency by calculating the number of colonies which can be transformed by 1 µg of plasmid DNA.

Transformation Efficiency = No. of transformants or colonies µg of plasmid DNA

How to Calculate Transformation Efficiency

Total volume of culture = 500 µl

No. of colonies obtained in 50 µl = 192

No. of colonies in 500 µl = (500 µl / 50 µl) × 192 = 1920 CFU

Concentration of Plasmid DNA = 10 µg/ml

Total volume of Plasmid DNA = 2 µl

Amount of Plasmid DNA in 2 µl = (2 µl × 10 µg) / 1000 µl = 2 × 10-2 µg

Transformation efficiency = 1920 CFU / (2 × 10-2 µg) = 9.60 × 104 CFU/µg

Restriction Enzymes:

These are D.N.A cutting enzyme found in Bacteria (and harvested from them for uses). Because they cut within the molecule, they are often called restriction endonucleases. A restriction enzyme recognizes and cut DNA only at particular sequence of nucleotide.

Who was the first one well understood?

In 1970, Hamilton Smith isolated Hind II (1st restriction enzymes well characterized and used for DNA clonings), which comes from Haemophilus influenzae.

How are they named?

They are named based on genus and species of bacteria it was isolated from (EcoR1 = Escherichia coli, Ry13).

How do they work?

They cut DNA by cleaving Phosphodiester bond (in sugar-phosphate backbone) that join adjacent nucleotides.

Specificity:

  • Restriction enzymes show specificity for certain substrates (DNA in this case).
  • They recognize, bind to, and cut DNA at specific sites called restriction sites (recognition sites).
  • Usually a 4-base pair or 6-base pair cutter.
  • Restriction sites / sequences are Palindromes (read same forward and backwards on opposite strands).

Restriction cuts:

  • Some cut on DNA to create fragments with overhanging single stranded end (sticky end or cohesive ends), while other create fragments with non-overhanging end (blunt ends).
  • Enzymes that create sticky ends are favored for cloning experiments since the D.N.A. fragments can be easily joined together.
  • (as long as DNA from any source can be digested as it has the specific restriction site).

Procedure for Restriction Digestion of lambda D.N.A.

Setting up the Restriction Digestion Reaction:

  1. Place the vial containing restriction enzymes (EcoR1 and Hind III) on ice.
  2. Thaw the vial containing substrate (lambda DNA) and 2x Assay Buffer.
  3. Prepare 2 different reaction mixture in 1.5 ml vials using the following components:

    Reaction 1 (EcoR1 digestion)

    • λDNA: 20 µl
    • 2x Assay Buffer: 25 µl
    • EcoR1: 3 µl

    Reaction 2 (Hind III digestion)

    • λDNA: 20 µl
    • 2x Assay Buffer: 25 µl
    • Hind III: 3 µl
  4. Incubate both the vials at 37°C for 1 hour.
  5. After an hour add 5 µl of Gel Loading Buffer to each of the vials to stop the digestion.
  6. Meanwhile, prepare a 1% agarose gel for electrophoresis.
  7. Load the digested sample, 10 µl of control DNA and 10 µl of λ/Hind III digest in different wells. Note down the order of loading.
  8. Electrophorese the sample at 50-100V for 1-2 hours.
  9. After electrophoresis, DNA sample can be visualized under UV transilluminator.

Nobel Prize and usage:

  • In 1978, the Nobel Prize for Medicine was awarded to Werner Arber, Daniel Nathans, and Hamilton Smith for their discovery of restriction endonucleases, which led to the development of recombinant DNA technology.
  • The first practical use of restriction enzymes in science and medicine was the manipulation of E. coli bacteria to express recombinant human insulin for the treatment of diabetics.
  • The restriction enzymes have been discovered in many different bacteria and other unicellular organisms. These restriction enzymes are able to scan along a length of DNA looking for a particular sequence of bases that they recognize.
