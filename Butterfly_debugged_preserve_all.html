pH metric Experiment Report

Date: 08/02/2025

Aim

To determine the pH value of different soil samples collected from various locations within the campus using pH paper and a digital pH meter.

Apparatus

  • Digital pH Meter
  • Glass Electrode
  • Beakers (100 mL)
  • Glass Rod / Stirrer
  • Analytical Weighing Balance
  • Sieve (2 mm mesh size)
  • Measuring Cylinder
  • Wash Bottle
  • Whatman Filter Paper (optional)

Chemicals Required

  • Standard Buffer Solutions (pH 4.0, pH 7.0, and pH 9.2 or 10.0)
  • Distilled Water / Deionized Water

Theory

Soil pH is a crucial chemical property that indicates the acidity or alkalinity of the soil matrix. It is defined as the negative logarithm (base 10) of hydrogen ion activity ($a_{H^+}$) in the soil solution:

$ text{pH} = - log_{10}[H^+]$

Soil pH controls nutrient availability, microbial activity, and plant growth. While pH paper gives a quick, rough estimation by changing color based on indicators, a digital pH meter provides highly accurate, precise measurements by checking the potential difference between a glass electrode and a reference electrode immersed in the suspension.

Procedure

Part 1: Soil Sample Preparation

  1. Collect soil samples from designated locations and air-dry them thoroughly.
  2. Crush the dried soil clots and pass the sample through a 2 mm sieve to remove gravel and organic debris.
  3. Weigh 20 grams of the sieved soil sample into a clean 100 mL beaker.
  4. Add 20 mL of distilled water to maintain a 1:1 soil-to-water ratio suspension.
  5. Stir the suspension vigorously with a glass rod for 10 to 15 minutes, then let it stand for 30 minutes to allow the soil particles to settle.

Part 2: pH Meter Setup & Calibration

  1. Switch on the digital pH meter and allow it to warm up for 15 minutes.
  2. Rinse the glass electrode thoroughly with distilled water and gently blot it dry using clean tissue paper.
  3. Immerse the electrode into the standard pH 7.0 buffer solution and adjust the calibration knob until the display reads exactly 7.00.
  4. Remove the electrode, rinse it again with distilled water, and calibrate it using either pH 4.0 (for expected acidic samples) or pH 9.2/10.0 buffer (for expected alkaline samples).

Part 3: pH Measurement & Data Logging

  1. Dip a strip of pH paper into the supernatant liquid of the soil suspension, observe the color change, and record the approximate pH value by comparing it to the standard color chart.
  2. Insert the calibrated glass electrode directly into the partially settled soil suspension.
  3. Wait for the digital display on the pH meter to stabilize, then record the final precise pH value directly from the screen.
  4. Repeat the cleaning and measuring process for all other remaining soil samples.

Calculation

No manual mathematical calculations are required for this experiment. The digital pH meter functions on a potentiometric principle that measures the voltage difference between the reference and measuring electrodes. It automatically converts this electrical potential into a direct pH reading using the programmed Nernst equation parameters inside its microprocessor system.

Result & Conclusion

Data Analysis:

The experiment yielded a wide variation in chemical properties across campus locations. The pH paper readings provided a reliable qualitative baseline, which was successfully refined by the precise decimal figures produced by the digital pH meter.

  • The soil sample from the Archery Ground is highly toxic/acidic ($ text{pH} = 2.40$).
  • The soil near the Gautam Buddha Statue ($ text{pH} = 7.03$) and the Front Lawn ($ text{pH} = 7.35$) is optimal and neutral.
  • The samples collected near the National Flag ($ text{pH} = 8.35$), Canteen ($ text{pH} = 8.04$), and Library ($ text{pH} = 7.82$) exhibit distinct alkaline characteristics.

Final Conclusion:

The pH paper method serves as an efficient, quick field test, while the digital pH meter is essential for high-precision analytical work. Proper calibration of the pH meter ensures accurate measurement of soil acidity/alkalinity, critical for agricultural and horticultural soil health management.

Reaction

🧠 Theory & Mechanism

This reaction follows the principle of Free Radical Addition Polymerization. The step-by-step mechanism includes:

  1. Redox Initiation: Reaction between Mohr's salt (Fe²⁺) and Hydrogen Peroxide (H₂O₂) produces highly reactive Hydroxyl Free Radicals (•OH).
    Fe²⁺ + H₂O₂ → Fe³⁺ + •OH + OH⁻
  2. Chain Propagation: The •OH free radical attacks the double bond of the Acrylamide monomer (CH₂=CH-CONH₂), forming a new monomer radical that rapidly propagates to form a long Polyacrylamide chain.
    3. Concentration Effect: Higher monomer concentration increases the rate of propagation, leading to a faster increase in viscosity. Precipitation: Polyacrylamide is highly soluble in water. When added to Isopropyl Alcohol containing a few drops of acid, Polyacrylamide becomes insoluble and precipitates as a white fibrous/gel-like solid.

📝 Procedure

  1. Step 1 Prepare 3 distinct aqueous solutions of Acrylamide in separate beakers:
    • Beaker 1: 1 g Acrylamide + Water
    • Beaker 2: 2 g Acrylamide + Water
    • Beaker 3: 8–10 g Acrylamide + Water
  2. Step 2 Add 0.08 g Mohr's Salt to each beaker and stir thoroughly using a glass rod until completely dissolved.
  3. Step 3 Add 5 ml H₂O₂ to each solution while stirring continuously. Leave the beakers undisturbed after a few seconds of stirring.
    Observation: Beaker 3 (highest concentration) thickens first. (Viscosity Order: Beaker 3 > Beaker 2 > Beaker 1)
  4. Step 4 After 25–30 minutes, prepare 100 ml Isopropyl Alcohol with a few drops of Conc. HCl in a separate 200 ml beaker and place it on a magnetic stirrer.
  5. Step 5 Under vigorous magnetic stirring, add the polymer solution from Beaker 3 drop-wise into the alcohol solution. Repeat the same process for Beakers 2 and 1.

Aqueous solution of formaldehyde is known as formalin (37 to 39% solution). Formaldehyde is oxidised to formic acid by an excess of iodine in an alkaline solution. The unreacted/excess iodine is then acidified and titrated against a standard solution of sodium thiosulphate using starch as an indicator.

  • I₂ + 2NaOH → NaOI + NaI + H₂O
  • NaOI + NaI + 2HCl → 2NaCl + I₂ + H₂O
  • I₂ + 2Na₂S₂O₃ → Na₂S₄O₆ + 2NaI

A titration based on the formation of a coordination complex is known as a complexometric titration. Lab setup

📸 Chemical Reactions & Molecular Structure:

Methyl Orange Structure

Chemical Reactions

💡 Theory:

  • Type of Titration: This is a multi-stage Acid-Base Neutralization Titration.
  • Polyprotic Nature: Phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) is a weak tribasic acid that releases three H⁺ ions in distinct steps.
  • First Equivalence Point: Neutralization to NaH₂PO₄ occurs at an acidic pH range around 4.7.
  • Role of Methyl Orange: It changes color from red to yellow around pH 3.1–4.4, making it ideal to detect the first proton neutralization step.
  • Role of Phenolphthalein: It changes from colorless to pink around pH 8.2–10.0, used separately to standardize the NaOH base against oxalic acid.

💡 Uses & Societal/Environmental Relevance:

  • Water Quality Monitoring: Helps detect excessive phosphate runoffs from agricultural fields into water bodies.
  • Environmental Protection: Aids in preventing eutrophication, which leads to algal blooms and oxygen depletion in aquatic ecosystems.
  • Industrial Control: Crucial for checking phosphoric acid levels in food preservation, fertilizers, and beverage manufacturing.

Experiment 01: Salinity Measurement by Argentometric Titration

Date: 24/08/24

🧪 Laboratory Experiment Report

Department of Chemistry

🎯 Experiment Aim

Measurement of salinity in a given water sample by simple titration method using AgNO₃ as an intermediate solution and Potassium Chromate (K₂CrO₄) as an indicator.

🔬 Apparatus Required

  • 🧪 Volumetric Flask (100 mL)
  • 📏 Burette with Ring Stand
  • 🧪 Conical Flask
  • 🥛 Beakers (100 mL & 250 mL)
  • 🧪 Measuring Cylinder
  • 💧 Wash Bottle (Distilled Water)
  • 🥢 Glass Stirrer
  • 🔴 Pipette Sucker / Bulbs

⚗️ Chemicals Required

  • Silver Nitrate (AgNO₃): Used as the intermediate standard titrant solution.
  • Potassium Chromate (K₂CrO₄): Primary indicator used for detecting the end-point.
  • Sodium Chloride (NaCl): Primary standard compound for standardizing the AgNO₃ solution.
  • Water Sample: Analyte containing unknown chloride/salinity concentration.
  • Distilled Water: For preparing all chemical solutions and glassware washing.

🔬 Titration Apparatus Assembly (Burette & Flask):

📚 Theoretical Background

💡 What Happens in Argentometric Titration

Argentometric titration involves precipitation reactions where silver ions ( text{Ag}^+ ) react with chloride ions ( text{Cl}^- ) to form an insoluble white precipitate of Silver Chloride ( ( text{AgCl} )). This specific method is widely recognized as Mohr's Method. It is a direct titration process where silver nitrate is titrated against the sample solution.

⭐ Significance & Application

Salinity is a fundamental parameter in environmental testing, water quality monitoring, and marine biology. High chloride levels cause severe corrosion in water pipelines and degrade agricultural quality. This experiment provides a very simple, fast, and cost-effective quantitative chemical technique without needing complex electronic meters.

⚡ Chemical Reactions & Reactions Involved

1. Main Chloride Precipitation Reaction:

$$ text{NaCl (aq)} + text{AgNO}_3 text{(aq)} rightarrow text{AgCl (s)} downarrow text{ (White ppt)} + text{NaNO}_3 text{(aq)}$$ $$ text{Net Ionic Equation: } text{Ag}^+ text{(aq)} + text{Cl}^- text{(aq)} rightarrow text{AgCl (s)} downarrow text{ [White Precipitate]}$$

2. End-Point Indicator Reaction:

$$ text{K}_2 text{CrO}_4 text{(aq)} + 2 text{AgNO}_3 text{(aq)} rightarrow text{Ag}_2 text{CrO}_4 text{(s)} downarrow text{ (Brick-Red ppt)} + 2 text{KNO}_3 text{(aq)}$$ $$ text{Net Ionic Equation: } 2 text{Ag}^+ text{(aq)} + text{CrO}_4^{2-} text{(aq)} rightarrow text{Ag}_2 text{CrO}_4 text{(s)} downarrow text{ [Brick Red/Reddish-Brown Precipitate]}$$

📋 Experimental Procedure

🔹 Part A: Preparation of Standard Solution

  1. Place a clean weighing paper on an electronic analytical balance and record the paper weight.
  2. Carefully add primary standard ( text{NaCl} ) onto the paper until the desired mass is reached. Record the combined total weight.
  3. Transfer the ( text{NaCl} ) salt into a clean 100 mL volumetric flask.
  4. Re-weigh the empty paper to measure the "after transfer weight" to eliminate residual particle error.
  5. Add ~40 mL distilled water, swirl till dissolved completely, and dilute up to the 100 mL graduation mark.

🔹 Part B: Titration Procedure

  1. Rinse and fill the burette with intermediate ( text{AgNO}_3 ) solution. Fix it to the ring stand.

4. THEORY & BACKEND MECHANISM

The synthesis of acetanilide is a classic example of an acetylation reaction, which falls under nucleophilic acyl substitution. Aniline acts as an aliphatic/aromatic primary amine nucleophile, attacking the carbonyl carbon of the glacial acetic acid acetylating agent.

C6H5NH2 (Aniline) + CH3COOH (Glacial Acetic Acid) → C6H5NHCOCH3 (Acetanilide) + H2O

Mechanism and Chemical Logic:

  • Role of Zinc Dust: Zinc dust acts as a crucial reducing agent. Aniline is highly susceptible to atmospheric oxidation, turning into dark brown or black impurities. Zinc prevents this oxidative degradation during prolonged heating, ensuring a clean, high-purity white product. It also reacts with trace acetic acid to generate micro-bubbles of hydrogen gas that assist in gentle agitation.
  • Role of Glacial Acetic Acid: It serves both as a reactant and the reaction solvent medium. Since water is produced as a byproduct, using an anhydrous (glacial) form shifts the chemical equilibrium forward via Le Chatelier's principle.

Why Use a Sand Bath instead of an Open Flame or Water Bath?

A Sand Bath is explicitly deployed in this experiment for two main chemical safety and thermodynamic reasons:

  1. Uniform Heat Distribution: Aniline and glacial acetic acid mixtures are susceptible to localized overheating, decomposition, and severe "bumping". Sand acts as a thermal buffer, evenly surrounding the spherical base of the round-bottom flask to distribute kinetic energy systematically.
  2. High Boiling Threshold: The boiling point of pure Glacial Acetic Acid is ~118°C, and Aniline is ~184°C. A standard water bath maxes out at 100°C and cannot supply the required activation energy over a 45-minute reflux period. The sand bath easily sustains temperatures above 120°C without risk of thermal cracking associated with a direct naked Bunsen burner flame hitting cold glass.

5. PROCEDURE

Part A: Condenser Setup & Reaction Mixture Preparation

  1. Clean and dry a 100 mL round-bottom flask thoroughly.
  2. Accurately measure and transfer 1.0 mL of Aniline and 2.0 mL of Glacial Acetic Acid into the flask.
  3. Add 1.0 g of fine Zinc dust to the liquid solution.
  4. Drop 2-3 small pieces of pumice stones (boiling chips) to ensure calm, controlled boiling throughout the session.
  5. Securely clamp the flask onto an iron stand over a pre-arranged sand bath sitting on a tripod stand.
  6. Vertically fit a reflux water condenser into the mouth of the round-bottom flask. Connect the lower inlet to a cold-water tap and run the upper outlet hose to the sink drainage line.

Part B: Refluxing and Reaction Phase

  1. Turn on the cold water flow slowly to ensure a full, continuous cooling jacket in the condenser.
  2. Ignite the Bunsen burner underneath the sand bath, applying constant heat to bring the mixture to a steady boil.
  3. Maintain this active state of refluxing for exactly 45 minutes. Watch the vapor line rise up into the lower third of the condenser and safely drip back down into the boiling vessel.

Part C: Isolation of Crude Acetanilide

  1. After 45 minutes, extinguish the burner flame and allow the flask to naturally cool down to room temperature.
  2. Carefully pour the warm reaction mixture into a beaker containing approximately 40-50 mL of ice-cold distilled water while stirring continuously with a glass rod.
  3. Observe the immediate precipitation of solid white crude acetanilide flakes.
  4. Filter the solid using a Buchner funnel vacuum filtration setup or standard filter paper.
  5. Wash the collected residue thoroughly on the filter bed using cold distilled water to extract any remaining unreacted acetic acid. Drain completely.

Part D: Recrystallization & Melting Point Identification

  1. Transfer the crude product to a fresh beaker and dissolve it in a minimum volume of boiling hot distilled water.
  2. Filter the boiling solution quickly through a fluted filter paper to discard unreacted metallic zinc residue.
  3. Set the clear filtrate aside to cool down unassisted. As the temperature drops, beautiful, pure, white needle-like crystals of acetanilide will reconstitute.
  4. Collect the purified crystals by filtration, press dry between sheets of filter paper, and place them inside a laboratory desiccator or air oven.
  5. Pack a tiny amount of the completely dried crystal powder into a sealed glass capillary tube and determine its physical melting point threshold using a standard melting point apparatus.

Triglyceride (Oil) + 3 C2H5OH (Ethanol)  →KOH  Glycerol + 3 FAEE (Biodiesel)

3. Theory & Background Chemistry

Enthalpy of Neutralization (ΔHneut) is defined as the heat evolved when one gram equivalent of an acid is completely neutralized by one gram equivalent of a base in dilute solution.

H+(aq) + Cl-(aq) + Na+(aq) + OH-(aq) → Na+(aq) + Cl-(aq) + H2O(l) + Heat

    Standard Operating Procedure (SOP)
    Qualitative Inorganic Salt Analysis

    ⚠️ CRITICAL WARNING FOR JUNIORS:
    1. NEVER THROW ANY SOLUTION/FILTRATE until the test is officially complete! The solution you are about to discard might be the mother liquor required for Cation Group testing!
    2. Always point the mouth of the test tube AWAY from yourself and your lab mates while heating.
    3. Smell gases by fanning/wafting the vapors gently toward your nose with your hand. Do NOT sniff directly from the test tube opening!

    1. Lab Discipline & Test Tube Handling Protocols

    📌 Golden Rules of Handling Test Tubes:
    • Dry Test Tube vs Wet Test Tube: Dry tests (like Flame test or Heating in a dry test tube) MUST use a completely moisture-free test tube. Moisture can decompose your salt prematurely!
    • Test Tube Holder: Clamp the holder at the upper 1/3rd section of the test tube, never at the top rim or middle.
    • Heating Protocol: Heat gently at a 45-degree angle while shaking slightly. Boiling liquid abruptly causes "bumping" which sprays boiling acid on your face.
    • Water Bath Usage: Organic solvents or reaction mixtures producing volatile gases must be heated in a boiling water bath, NOT over a direct flame.

    2. Systematic Anion Identification (Negative Ion)

    Anion detection follows a 3-Step Elimination Strategy. Work systematically from Group I to Group III.

    GROUP I: Dilute H₂SO₄ Group (CO₃²⁻, S²⁻, SO₃²⁻, NO₂⁻)

    Action: Take 0.1g salt in a clean test tube + Add 1-2 mL Dilute H₂SO₄ (No heating initially, then mild warm).

    • CO₃²⁻ (Carbonate): Effervescence with colorless, odorless gas (CO₂). Turns Lime Water milky.
    • S²⁻ (Sulfide): Rotten egg smell gas (H₂S). Turns Lead Acetate paper Black.
    • SO₃²⁻ (Sulfite): Suffocating burning sulfur smell gas (SO₂). Turns Acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ paper Green.
    • NO₂⁻ (Nitrite): Light brown vapors (NO₂) on mild heating. Turns starch-iodide paper Blue.
    GROUP II: Concentrated H₂SO₄ Group (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻, NO₃⁻, CH₃COO⁻, C₂O₄²⁻)

    Action: Take 0.1g dry salt in a DRY test tube + Add 1 mL Conc. H₂SO₄. Heat gently.

    • Cl⁻ (Chloride): Colorless pungent gas (HCl) giving dense white fumes with an NH₄OH dipped glass rod. Requires Chromyl Chloride Confirmation
    • Br⁻ (Bromide): Reddish-brown vapors (Br₂). Intensity increases on adding MnO₂ powder.
    • I⁻ (Iodide): Deep Violet/Purple vapors (I₂). Turns starch paper blue.
    • NO₃⁻ (Nitrate): Pungent brown gas (NO₂) on strong heating. Intensity explodes on adding Paper Pellets/Copper turnings!
    • CH₃COO⁻ (Acetate): Vinegar-like smell. Confirmed by Ester test (Ethanol + Conc. H₂SO₄ → Fruity smell).
    GROUP III: Independent / Special Anions (SO₄²⁻, PO₄³⁻, BO₃³⁻)

    These anions do not give specific gases with acids. They require wet tests from Water Extract (W.E.) or Soda Extract (S.E.).

    • SO₄²⁻ (Sulfate): Water Extract + BaCl₂ Solution → White Precipitate (BaSO₄) insoluble in Conc. HCl.
    • PO₄³⁻ (Phosphate): Soda Extract + Conc. HNO₃ + Ammonium Molybdate → Canary Yellow Precipitate on warming.
    • BO₃³⁻ (Borate): Salt + Conc. H₂SO₄ + Ethanol in porcelain dish → Ignite → Green Edge Flame.

    3. Masterclass: Chromyl Chloride Test (Cl⁻ Confirmation)

    ⚠️ WHY JUNIORS FAIL THIS TEST: They add water inside the primary reaction test tube OR let the red gas escape into the air. Read the exact 4-step procedure below!
    Exact Step-by-Step Procedure:
    1. Primary Tube: Mix Dry Salt (0.1g) + Solid K₂Cr₂O₇ powder (0.2g) in a 100% DRY test tube. Add 3-4 drops of Conc. H₂SO₄.
    2. Delivery Setup: Heat gently. Deep Red Vapors of Chromyl Chloride (CrO₂Cl₂) will evolve.
    3. Absorption Tube: Pass these red vapors into a SECOND test tube containing 2 mL NaOH solution. The NaOH solution turns Yellow due to Na₂CrO₄ formation.
    4. Final Acidification: Acidify the yellow NaOH solution with Dilute Acetic Acid (CH₃COOH) and add 1 mL Lead Acetate solution.
    5. Observation: Bright Yellow Precipitate (PbCrO₄) confirms Chloride (Cl⁻).

    4. The Brain-Melting Issue: Interfering Anions Removal

    What is an Interfering Anion?
    Anions like Oxalate (C₂O₄²⁻), Phosphate (PO₄³⁻), Fluoride (F⁻), and Borate (BO₃³⁻) form insoluble complex precipitates with Group III/IV/V Cations when the medium is made alkaline with NH₄OH!
    Result? They cause false positive precipitations early in Group 3, destroying your cation detection!
    Protocol to Remove Interfering Anions (Executed AFTER Group II Cations):
    1. If Group II is absent, take the filtrate / Original Solution (O.S.).
    2. Boil off all H₂S gas completely (test with Lead Acetate paper; it must NOT turn black).
    3. Add 1 mL Conc. HNO₃ and boil (to oxidize Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺).
    4. Add solid NH₄Cl and NH₄OH till alkaline. If Phosphate is present, add Zirconium Nitrate or FeCl₃ solution in buffered acetic acid medium to precipitate out Phosphate as Iron/Zirconium phosphate.
    5. Filter the precipitate out. Use the clear FILTRATE for standard Group III Cation analysis!

    Dissolve salt in Water. If insoluble, try Dilute HCl. If still insoluble, warm in Conc. HCl. DO NOT use H₂SO₄ or HNO₃ to make O.S.!

    Organic Compound & Functional Group Analysis

    ⚠️ SAFETY WARNING FOR ORGANIC LAB:

    A. Physical State, Odor & Ignition Test (Aromatic vs Aliphatic)
    • Ignition Test: Heat a small amount of compound on a nickel spatula directly over Bunsen flame.
      • Sooty/Black Smoke Flame: Aromatic Compound (high C:H ratio).
      • Non-Sooty/Clean Blue-Yellow Flame: Aliphatic Compound.
    • Characteristic Odors: Bitter almonds (Nitrobenzene), Fishy smell (Amines), Fruity smell (Esters), Vinegar smell (Carboxylic Acids), Phenolic smell (Phenols).

    • 1. Bromine Water / CCl₄ Test: Dissolve compound in 1 mL CCl₄ + add Bromine water dropwise.

      👉 Observation: Discharge of purple color with brown ppt (MnO₂) indicates Unsaturation.

    2. Sodium Fusion Extract (Lassaigne's Test for Extra Elements)

    Required to detect Nitrogen (N), Sulfur (S), and Halogens (Cl, Br, I).

    🧪 How to Prepare Lassaigne's Extract (L.E.):
    1. Fuse a pea-sized dry piece of clean Sodium metal in an ignition tube until it melts into a shiny bead.
    2. Add a pinch of organic compound and heat strongly until the tube becomes red hot.
    3. Plunge the red-hot tube into a china dish containing 10-15 mL distilled water and crush it.
    4. Boil the solution for 5 minutes and FILTER IT. Clear filtrate = Sodium Fusion Extract (L.E.).
    DO NOT DISCARD THIS FILTRATE till elemental analysis is finished!
    Element Test Procedure on Lassaigne's Extract (L.E.) Observation & Confirmation
    Nitrogen (N) 2 mL L.E. + freshly prepared FeSO₄ solution + Boil + add 2-3 drops FeCl₃ + acidify with Conc. HCl. Prussian Blue Color / Precipitate confirms Nitrogen.
    Sulfur (S) 2 mL L.E. + 2 drops freshly prepared Sodium Nitroprusside solution. Brilliant Purple / Violet Coloration confirms Sulfur.
    Both N & S 2 mL L.E. + FeCl₃ solution (without adding FeSO₄). Blood Red Coloration (due to Fe(SCN)₃ formation).

    Pale Yellow ppt sparingly soluble in NH₄OH = Br⁻
    Bright Yellow ppt insoluble in NH₄OH = I⁻

    3. Systematic Functional Group Identification Hierarchy

    Follow the tests in exact chronological order to eliminate groups smoothly without misinterpretation.

    STEP 1: Test for Carboxylic Acid (-COOH)
    • Sodium Bicarbonate Test: Add organic compound to 1 mL saturated NaHCO₃ solution.
      👉 Observation: Brisk effervescence with evolution of colorless, odorless CO₂ gas = Carboxylic Acid Confirmed.
    • Ester Test (Confirmation): Compound + Ethanol + 2 drops Conc. H₂SO₄ → Heat in water bath → Pour into Na₂CO₃ solution.
      👉 Observation: Pleasant Fruity smell confirms Acid.
    STEP 2: Test for Phenolic Group (-OH Phenolic)
    • Neutral Ferric Chloride (FeCl₃) Test: Organic compound in water/alcohol + 2-3 drops fresh neutral FeCl₃.
      👉 Observation: Intense Violet, Green, or Blue coloration confirms Phenol.
    • Phthalein Reaction Test: Phenol + Phthalic anhydride + 2 drops Conc. H₂SO₄ → Heat 1 min → Cool → Pour into excess dilute NaOH.
      👉 Observation: Intense Pink / Red color confirms Phenol.
    STEP 3: Carbonyl Group (>C=O) - Aldehydes & Ketones

    General Carbonyl Test: Compound + 2,4-DNP Reagent → Shake vigorously.
    👉 Observation: Yellow, Orange, or Red crystalline precipitate confirms Carbonyl group.

    Differentiating Aldehyde vs Ketone:
    • Tollens' Silver Mirror Test (Aldehydes ONLY): Compound + Fresh Tollens' Reagent → Warm in water bath for 5 mins.
      👉 Observation: Shining Silver Mirror formed on test tube walls = Aldehyde (-CHO).
    • Fehling's Test (Aliphatic Aldehydes ONLY): Compound + Fehling A & B (equal volume) → Boil in water bath.
      👉 Observation: Brick Red precipitate of Cu₂O = Aliphatic Aldehyde.
    • If 2,4-DNP is (+ve) BUT Tollens' & Fehling's are (-ve): The compound is a Ketone (>C=O). Confirmed by m-Dinitrobenzene Test (Violet color with NaOH).
    STEP 4: Test for Amino Group (-NH₂ / Amine)
    • Litmus Paper Test: Amines are basic in nature; they turn red litmus paper Blue.
    • Azo Dye Test (Primary Aromatic Amines ONLY):
      1. Dissolve compound in dilute HCl, cool in ice bath to 0-5°C.
      2. Add 2-3 drops aqueous NaNO₂ solution (Forms Diazonium salt).
      3. Pour this mixture into ice-cold alkaline $ beta$-naphthol solution.
      👉 Observation: Brilliant Orange / Red Dye precipitate confirms 1° Aromatic Amine (e.g., Aniline).
    STEP 5: Test for Alcohols (-OH) & Esters (-COOR)
    • Ceric Ammonium Nitrate Test (Alcohols): Compound + 1 mL Ceric Ammonium Nitrate reagent.
      👉 Observation: Rapid appearance of Red / Orange color confirms Alcohol.
    • Lucas Test (1° vs 2° vs 3° Alcohols): Compound + Lucas Reagent (Conc. HCl + Anhydrous ZnCl₂).
      👉 Immediate Turbidity: 3° Alcohol.
      👉 Turbidity in 5-10 mins: 2° Alcohol.
      👉 No Turbidity on cold condition: 1° Alcohol.
    • Hydroxamic Acid Test (Esters): Compound + Hydroxylamine HCl + NaOH → Warm → Cool → Acidify with HCl + FeCl₃.
      👉 Observation: Deep Magenta / Purple color confirms Ester.

    4. Quick Troubleshooting & Elimination Guide

    • ❌ False Positive Bicarbonate Test? Some nitrophenols are strongly acidic and give faint effervescence. Cross-check with Neutral FeCl₃!
    • ❌ FeCl₃ color disappearing? Excess FeCl₃ or alcoholic solution destroys the complex color. Always use Fresh, Neutral FeCl₃ dropwise!
    • ❌ Azo-dye test failing? Temperature MUST be strictly maintained between 0–5°C using ice bath; above 10°C diazonium salt decomposes into phenol!
    • ❌ Silver mirror not forming? Dirty/greasy test tube prevents silver deposition. Clean with NaOH first, then HNO₃!
